A B S T R A C T   
Mothers exposed to infections during pregnancy disproportionally birth children who develop autism and schizophrenia, disorders associated with altered GABAergic function. The maternal immune activation (MIA) model recapitulates this risk factor, with many studies also reporting disruptions to GABAergic interneuron expression, protein, cellular density and function. However, it is unclear if there are species, sex, age, region, or GABAergic subtype specific vulnerabilities to MIA. Furthermore, to fully comprehend the impact of MIA on the GABAergic system a synthesised account of molecular, cellular, electrophysiological and behavioural findings was required. To this end we conducted a systematic review of GABAergic interneuron changes in the MIA model, focusing on the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus. We reviewed 102 articles that revealed robust changes in a number of GABAergic markers that present as gestationally-specific, region-specific and sometimes sex-specific. Disruptions to GABAergic markers coincided with distinct behavioural phenotypes, including memory, sensorimotor gating, anxiety, and sociability. Findings suggest the MIA model is a valid tool for testing novel therapeutics designed to recover GABAergic function and associated behaviour.   
1. Introduction 
Infection during pregnancy is a well-known risk factor for the unborn child to develop schizophrenia or autism spectrum disorder (ASD) later in life (Brown, 2012; Estes and McAllister, 2016a), with some recent evidence also implicating maternal infection as a risk factor for major depressive (Class, 2019; Gilman et al., 2016) and bipolar disorders (Class, 2019; Canetta et al., 2014; Parboosing et al., 2013). Initially identified through epidemiological studies of past influenza (O’Calla-ghan et al., 1994) and rubella epidemics (Buka et al., 2001), this led to a major area of translational research focused on understanding the molecular and neurological mechanisms underpinning this risk. With the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic the need to understand the impact of maternal SARS-CoV-2 infection on infant neurodevelopment is particularly urgent, as potentially millions of unborn children have been exposed on a global scale. While studies investigating the impact of maternal SARS-CoV-2 infection on foetal development remain ongoing 
(Hill et al., 2023a), early findings implicate elevated proinflammatory markers within the foetal compartment (Gee et al., 2021) and show an increase in the rate of neurodevelopmental disorders in infants exposed to SARS-CoV-2 in utero (Edlow et al., 2022), and altered methylation of genes belonging to neurodevelopment pathways (Hill et al., 2023b). Over decades of research on the increased risk for neurodevelopmental disorders in unborn children exposed to maternal infection, it is thought that the mechanism by which infection increases risk is driven by activation of the maternal immune system (Smith et al., 2007; Estes and McAllister, 2016b; Brown and Meyer, 2018; Solek et al., 2018). 
Maternal immune activation is frequently modelled in pregnant rodents by administering the immunostimulatory agents Lipopolysaccha-ride (LPS) or Polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (Poly-I:C) (Meyer and Feldon, 2010; Meyer et al., 2009). These compounds allow maternal immune activation (MIA) to be precisely timed to specific gestational windows of interest, are safer to handle than live pathogens, and approximate the immune response of a bacterial (Hoshino et al., 1999)

The popularity of research investigating MIA has exploded in the past 20 years, with 751 articles published in 2021 compared to 97 in 2000. This is likely due to the capacity of the MIA model to recapitulate many neurobiological and behavioural deficits associated with ASD and schizophrenia, and to allow for the testing of novel therapeutics in a preclinical setting (Meyer and Feldon, 2010; Meyer et al., 2009). Ro-dents exposed to MIA exhibit differences in the Gamma-Aminobutyric acid (GABA)ergic system that are reminiscent of schizophrenia (Torrey et al., 2005; Hashimoto et al., 2008a; Thompson Ray et al., 2011; Gui-dotti et al., 2000a) and ASD (Fatemi et al., 2002; Bauman and Kemper, 2005; Hashemi et al., 2017) with particularly strong parallels for interneurons expressing either somatostatin (SST) or parvalbumin (PV) (Handunnetthi et al., 2021). As perturbed GABAergic signalling has been functionally implicated in the symptomology of both conditions, understanding how MIA contributes to GABAergic dysfunction, and determining if GABAergic dysfunction can be normalised by prospective therapeutics, is of high translational relevance. 
1.1. GABAergic system dysfunction in autism and schizophrenia 
GABA is produced by the enzymes Glutamate decarboxylase (GAD) 67 and GAD65 (Asada et al., 1997), and functions as the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the adult brain. Reductions in GAD65 and GAD67 within the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and hippocampus are one of the most well replicated post mortem findings in schizophrenia (Torrey et al., 2005; Hashimoto et al., 2008a; Thompson Ray et al., 2011; Guidotti et al., 2000a), and reductions have also been reported in the parietal cortex and cerebellum in ASD (Fatemi et al., 2002; Bauman and Kemper, 2005; Yip et al., 2007). Neurons expressing GAD65 and GAD67 are termed GABAergic interneurons, and are often subdivided based on their unique morphology, specific targets (e.g. pyramidal cells or other interneurons), electrophysiological output, and the expression of specific markers such as neuropeptides, Ca2+ binding proteins, ion channels, receptors and transporters (Le Magueresse and Monyer, 2013). Two interneuron subtypes of particular interest are those expressing either the calcium binding protein parvalbumin (PV), or the neuropeptide somatostatin (SST). Reductions in both subtypes have been consistently reported in schizophrenia (Hashimoto et al., 2008a; Beasley et al., 2002; Hashimoto et al., 2003a; Zhang and Reynolds, 2002; Morris et al., 2008), and are of considerable interest as potential targets for novel therapeutics, owing to their putative roles in the regulation of cognitive and social function (Perez et al., 2019; van Heukelum et al., 2019; Kahn and Keefe, 2013). While reductions in SST+ and PV+ interneurons have been reported in ASD (Hashemi et al., 2017; Zikopoulos and Barbas, 2013), this body of work remains comparatively small, likely owing to a dominant hypothesis of ASD as primarily a disorder of abnormal connectivity (Coghlan et al., 2012) and greater limitations on the availability of human post-mortem tissue. However, given the substantial overlap in developmental risk factors between ASD and schizophrenia, and shared deficits in sociability (Brüne, 2005; Frye, 2018; Tordjman et al., 2019) and some cognitive domains (Øie et al., 2020; Eack et al., 2013; Marinopoulou et al., 2016), it is plausible that some symptoms are mediated through common neurobiological abnormalities, such as disruptions to PV+ and SST+ interneurons within the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and hippocampus. 
1.1.1. Differences between MIA models and the need for a systematic review 
While post-mortem findings have been recapitulated in rodents exposed to MIA in some studies, substantial heterogeneity has emerged between laboratories, which may be due to differences in experimental design. For instance, while the behavioural and neurobiological impacts of MIA appears driven by general inflammation, most notably though the actions of IL-17 (Choi et al., 2016) and Il-6 (Smith et al., 2007), LPS 
and Poly-I:C induce distinct innate immune responses, with LPS inducing an antibacterial response through activation of Toll like receptor(TLR)4, and poly-I:C inducing an antiviral immune response through TLR3 (Bao et al., 2022). As such, differences in the inflammatory cascades between LPS and Poly-I:C may lead to subtle changes in experimental outcomes, and differentially reflect various types of maternal infections: for example, bacterial infections such as toxoplasma gondii are best modelled by LPS while influenza and SARS-Cov-2 being viral are best modelled by Poly-I:C (Bao et al., 2022). Another factor that may impact experimental outcomes is differences in the gestational timepoint of exposure, which are associated with distinct developmental processes. For instance, SST+ and PV+ interneurons are born within the medial ganglionic eminence (MGE) in mid gestation and mid to late gestation respectively (Llorca and Deogracias, 2022), before undergoing an extended tangential migration to their final positions within the cortical and hippocampal anlage, with the hippocampus one of the last and most distal locations settled (Marín and Rubenstein, 2001). MIA during periods associated with the birth and early differentiation of GABAergic interneurons may disrupt these processes, while MIA in later gestation may lead to disruptions in migration, and the integration of neurons into nascent circuits once they reach their final location. Differences in the sensitivity of transcription factors that guide the proliferation, differentiation and migration of distinct GABAergic subtypes to MIA may also contribute to differences observed between GABAergic subpopulations. For example, our lab has recently reported that exposure to MIA in late gestation is associated with changes in Arx (Nakamura et al., 2019), while targeted exposure at GD12–15 leads to changes in Nkx2.1 (Nakamura et al., 2022). Other factors that appear to influence findings include batch differences between immunogenic agents (Kowash et al., 2019; Careaga et al., 2018) and differences between males and females (Braun et al., 2019), among others. This was addressed by the 2017 American College of Neuropsychopharmacology (ACNP) conference, which developed a range of reporting guidelines authored by an expert panel to aid in the interpretation of studies investigating MIA (Kentner et al., 2019). However, while reporting standards are greatly improved by this initiative, the heterogeneous findings surrounding GABAergic dysfunction in the MIA model call for a systematic review to identify replicated findings that represent core phenotypes of this model, to guide future research towards current gaps in the literature, and to identify potential therapeutic targets related to GABAergic signalling. Furthermore, to better understand the impact of MIA across multiple GABAergic domains, including molecular, cellular, electrophysiology and behaviour, there was a need to converge findings from these distinct domains. 
2. Methods 
2.1. Protocol registration and design 
The systematic review protocol was published in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) registry on 27/ 05/2020 (registration number CRD42020181581). Submission and registration of the protocol occurred after preliminary searches were conducted, which were performed to assess the size of the literature and guide the creation of a predesigned excel spreadsheet for data extraction. Article screening and data extraction was performed after protocol registration on PROSPERO.

On the 15/04/2020, Pubmed was searched for journal articles

A second search using additional search terms was performed, and papers published between 15/05/2023, and 15/4/2020 were incorporated into to the review. 10 papers identified using google scholar were also incorporated into the review. 2.1.2. Data extraction 
The following information was extracted from each eligible journal 
article: Title, authors, journal of publication, immunogenic agent (LPS vs Poly-I:C), gestational day of injection, dose of agent, route of administration, agent manufacturer, batch number of immunogenic agent, species of animal model, strain of animal model. All reports relating to maternal temperature, weight, sickness behaviour, and cytokines were extracted. Additionally, all findings assessing in utero changes (either in the foetus, placenta, or amniotic fluid) were extracted. With regards to postnatal neurobiological findings in the offspring, sample size, sex, age of assessment, brain region, and method of assessment were recorded. Data was extracted from the main body text, figures, figure legends, and the supplementary information of all eligible 
articles. Behavioural outcomes were only extracted for articles containing findings relevant to GABAergic signalling. As such, the behavioural outcomes reported represent findings in a subset of papers that also investigated alterations in GABAergic expression, cell number or functioning. Behavioural outcomes were collapsed for narrative review across 5 outcome categories: cognition, anxiety, sociability, social memory, sensorimotor gating. Due to substantial heterogeneity in outcome measures and experimental conditions, no quantitative synthesis was performed. The data extraction spreadsheet is available upon request. Regarding data relating to high performance liquid chromatography or liquid chromatography mass spectrometry, only GABA levels were reported. For quality assessment, the SYCLE risk of bias tool was used. Quality assessment of each study was assessed by Brendan Gillespie and Sandesh Panthi.

3.1.1. SYRCLE risk of bias and appraisal of current experimental approaches 
The risk of bias arising from the potential influence of funders, differences in baseline characteristics between treatment groups, and differences in the gestational timing of administering saline, Poly-I:C, or LPS was typically low (Fig. 2). However, few studies provided the information necessary to assess other factors that may bias results. Similar findings have been reported for other systematic review using the SYRCLE risk of bias, and may reflect broader reporting standards in animal research (Jarvis and Williams, 2016; Macleod et al., 2015). While the ACNP reporting guidelines are an exciting advance in the integrity of MIA research (Kentner et al., 2019), we encourage the inclusion of additional questions to address if cages were placed randomly within the animal facility, if a study protocol was published, if animals were randomly selected for outcome assessment, and what methods were used to justify excluding animals from analysis. This would allow the MIA reporting guidelines to encapsulate all information required for future systematic reviews using the SYRCLE risk of bias, which is highly regarded and has rapidly gained popularity. Another factor that may confound results is the tendency of offspring from the same litter to exhibit similarities due to their common intrauterine environment, maternal care, and litter size (Lazic and Essioux, 2013). As the relevance of litter effects in the MIA model is controversial (Lazic, 2013; Giovanoli and Meyer, 2013), studies that assessed more than one offspring per litter and did not control for this effect were graded as unclear with respect to unit of analysis errors. To better characterise potential litter effects, we suggest the use of mixed effects models in future studies that assess more than one offspring per litter (Potter et al., 2023; Nakamura et al., 2021a). Controls for MIA were generally administered saline via the same route as the viral or bacterial mimetic. Substantial methodological information was generally reported for RT-PCR and behavioural assessments, and behavioural assessments typically adhered to standardised testing paradigms. In contrast, findings related to IHC were typically of limited reproducibility due to limited information related to 
microscope setup and image acquisition, and relied on two-dimensional cell counting methods that are considered less robust than stereological techniques (Boyce et al., 2010). Furthermore, studies using western blotting typically relied on a single housekeeping gene to as a loading control. While this is common practice, the use of total protein normalisation offers many comparative advantages and should be considered in the design of future projects (Ghosh et al., 2014). 
3.1.2. MIA alters transcription factors involved in GABAergic differentiation and migration 
Changes in transcription factors involved in GABAergic differentiation, migration and maturation were reported in seven studies. Three studies reported reduced expression of transcription factors associated with the Nkx2.1 signalling pathway in MIA exposed foetal brains, such as Nkx2.1 (Nakamura et al., 2019; Tsivion-Visbord et al., 2020), Lhx6 (Tsivion-Visbord et al., 2020; Oskvig et al., 2012), Dlx1 (Nakamura et al., 2019; Tsivion-Visbord et al., 2020; Oskvig et al., 2012), Dlx2 (Nakamura et al., 2019; Tsivion-Visbord et al., 2020; Oskvig et al., 2012) and Arx (Nakamura et al., 2019; Tsivion-Visbord et al., 2020; Oskvig et al., 2012), while one study reported increased expression of Nkx2.1, Lhx6, and Dlx1 (Lacaille et al., 2019). These factors regulate the proliferation, differentiation and migration of GABAergic interneurons derived from the medial ganglionic eminence (MGE) that ultimately express SST or PV (Xu et al., 2008). This may occur in part through epigenetic mechanisms, with many of these factors exhibiting differential expression and methylation in the PFC of adult offspring exposed to MIA (Richetto et al., 2017). This parallels alterations in the Nkx2.1 pathway in schizophrenia (Malt et al., 2016; Kathuria et al., 2020; Volk et al., 2012), and associations between mutations in ARX (Sherr, 2003) and multiple DLX genes (Liu et al., 2009; Poitras et al., 2010) with ASD. While one study reported that MIA was not associated with changes in the total number of Nkx2.1 + neuronal progenitors in the MGE (Lacaille et al., 2019), others have shown MIA impacts progenitor proliferation but this is dependent on the gestational time point. Reduced progenitor proliferation was shown 24 h after exposure at GD9.5 (Vasistha et al., 2020), with no changes 48 h after exposure at GD12.5 (Vasistha et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2022), and increased proliferation 24 h after exposure at GD16.5 (Vasistha et al., 2020). Reduced proliferation coincided with reduced numbers of GAD+ or Nkx2.1 + neurons in the developing foetal cortex (Lacaille et al., 2019; Vasistha et al., 2020), but were not associated with changes in GAD+ interneuron apoptosis (Lacaille et al., 2019; Vasistha et al., 2020). 
A particularly noteworthy study was Yu et al., who convincingly demonstrated that reduced proliferation of Nkx2.1 + progenitors in MIA exposed offspring may be driven by reduced expression of microglial GPR56, and that upregulating GPR56 recovered MIA induced reductions in the number of PV+ interneurons in adulthood (Yu et al., 2022).

3.1.3. MIA disrupts the GABAergic excitatory to inhibitory switch 
While GABA traditionally plays an inhibitory role in the adult 
mammalian brain, it acts as an excitatory neurotransmitter during prenatal and perinatal stages of mammalian neurodevelopment. The excitatory effects of GABA in immature neurons are thought to be due to 
increased expression of the Cl- importer NKCC1 and decreased expression of the Cl- exporter KCC2, leading to a higher intraneuronal concentration of Cl- relative to the extracellular space (Ben-Ari et al., 2007). During development, NKCC1 expression gradually declines and is replaced by increased KCC2. This results in a lower concentration of intracellular chloride relative to the extracellular space, leading to a net influx of Cl- ions upon the activation of GABAA receptors, and consequent hyperpolarisation. This excitatory to inhibitory switch occurs gradually over the perinatal period, with a transient, oxytocin mediated inhibitory shift at birth, which appears crucial for normal neurodevelopment (Tyzio et al., 2014) and potentially protects the foetus from obstetric complications. While not directly measurable, converging clinical evidence suggests that ASD and schizophrenia may be associated with disruptions to this inhibitory switch, as both conditions are associated with epilepsy (Lukmanji et al., 2019; Danielyan and Nasrallah, 2009) and functional mutations in KCC2 (Merner et al., 2015). Similarly, deficits in the GABAergic switch have also been reported in the valproic acid and fragile X mouse models of ASD (Tyzio et al., 2014; He et al., 2014) and in 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (Amin et al., 2017), one of the strongest genetic risk factors for ASD (Niklasson et al., 2009) and schizophrenia (Drew et al., 2011). 
The impact of MIA on the GABAergic shift was investigated in three studies. The first reported significantly increased NKCC1 expression and decreased KCC2 expression in adulthood, suggestive of a more immature GABAergic phenotype (Richetto et al., 2014). This was followed up by Corrondi et al., who reported increased NKCC1 and decreased KCC2 protein in MIA exposed mice at GD17, PD20, and PD90 (Corradini et al., 2018), and directly confirmed this immature phenotype in 20-day old offspring using chloride imaging and patch clamp techniques (Corradini et al., 2018). Interestingly, Corrondi et al. noted that primary neurons taken from MIA exposed foetuses at GD18 and cultured for 6 days in vitro demonstrated comparable alterations in KCC2, NKCC1, and intracellular chloride to primary neurons taken immediately from perinatal offspring exposed to MIA. As these cultured neurons were raised outside of the brain, this led the authors to conclude that MIA induces disruptions to the GABAergic shift that become ‘sculpted’ into the developmental program of foetal neurons and are independent of non-neuronal processes. 3.1.4. GAD65 and GAD67 expression in Autism and Schizophrenia 
One of the best replicated findings in schizophrenia is reduced 
GAD67 expression and protein in multiple brain regions, particularly in the hippocampus and dlPFC (Torrey et al., 2005; Hashimoto et al., 2008a; Thompson Ray et al., 2011; Guidotti et al., 2000a; Bullock et al., 2008; Purves-Tyson et al., 2021). GAD67 is encoded by the GAD1 gene, and is responsible for producing up to 90% of GABA (Asada et al., 1997). While the mechanisms underlying this reduction are not fully understood, alterations in GAD1 methylation (Huang and Akbarian, 2007; Ruzicka et al., 2015), histone modification (Huang et al., 2007), and chromatin structure (Bharadwaj et al., 2013) have been implicated, however these findings are not always consistent (Richetto and Meyer, 2021). Similarly, candidate gene approaches have identified multiple schizophrenia associated SNP’s in GAD1 (Addington et al., 2005; Zhao et al., 2007), however these SNP’s were not reflected in GWAS studies (Pardiñas et al., 2018; Trubetskoy et al., 2022) or replicated by other candidate gene studies (Zhang et al., 2021a). These inconsistencies likely reflect the heterogenous and syndromic nature of schizophrenia, and may indicate that a variety of pathogenic mechanisms converge on reduced GAD67 function, which in turn induces a common clinical phenotype. Indeed, altered GABAergic signalling within the PFC and hippocampus has been implicated in multiple symptom domains of schizophrenia, including social deficits (Perez et al., 2019; van Heuke-lum et al., 2019), cognitive deficits (Kahn and Keefe, 2013), and psychotic symptoms (Grace, 2012). The second major enzyme contributing to GABA production is GAD65, which is predominantly localised to neuronal terminals (Kaufman et al., 1991), and produces GABA during periods of high demand (Martin and Rimvall, 1993). While some studies have reported alterations in GAD65 in schizophrenia (Hashimoto et al., 2008b; Dracheva et al., 2004), the majority have reported no differences (Huang et al., 2007; Glausier et al., 2015; Heckers et al., 2002; Guidotti et al., 2000b; Hashimoto et al., 2008c). While reductions in GAD65 and GAD67 have been reported in the parietal cortex and cerebellar cortices in ASD (Fatemi et al., 2002; Yip et al., 2007) (however, see (Yip et al., 2008)), to the best of our knowledge, this has yet to be assessed in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) or hippocampus. 
3.1.5. Altered GAD65 and GAD67 in the Juvenile or Adolescent Cortex Following MIA is Layer Specific 
Studies generally reported no gross alterations in GAD65 or GAD67 expression (Richetto et al., 2014, 2013; Deslauriers et al., 2013; Cassella et al., 2016; Potter et al., 2022) or protein (Richetto et al., 2014) within the PFC of juvenile and adolescent rodents (Table 1), with one exception (Deslauriers et al., 2013). However, one study did report that the expression of GAD65 was reduced in offspring raised by an MIA exposed mother, but not prenatally exposed to MIA, suggesting that maternal care may be a significant factor in its regulation (Richetto et al., 2013). Changes in the number of GAD65 + and GAD67 + appeared layer 
dependent, with reduced GAD65 + neuronal density reported in layer 1 of the anterior cingulate and layer 5 of the prelimbic cortex, but no differences in two cohorts where cortical layers were not stratified (Lacaille et al., 2019). Reductions in the density of GAD67 + neurons were also reported in layers I-III of the prelimbic cortex, while no significant differences were reported in the anterior cingulate cortex (Lacaille et al., 2019). 
3.1.6. MIA is associated with reduced GAD67 expression in a subregion specific manner within the adult PFC 
Within the PFC four studies investigating adult mice reported comparable reductions in GAD67 expression (Nakamura et al., 2019; Richetto et al., 2014, 2013; Labouesse et al., 2015), which appears to be driven by prenatal exposure to MIA, rather than changes in maternal behaviour (Richetto et al., 2013) (Table 1). Similarly, the majority of studies reported reductions in GAD67 protein across multiple subregions in the mPFC (Richetto et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2019; Canetta et al., 2016), with some exceptions (Nakamura et al., 2019; Li et al., 2015). Inter-estingly, Cannetta et al. reported reduced staining for GAD67 within PV+ cells, mirroring findings of reduced GAD67 expression in PV+ cells in people with schizophrenia (Canetta et al., 2016). Studies investigating rats indicated region specific effects, with only one report indicating a reduction in GAD67 expression in the prelimbic cortex (Cassella et al., 2016) and no changes in other cortical regions such as the cingulate, and auditory cortex (Cassella et al., 2016; Rahman et al., 2020; Duchatel et al., 2019). The majority of studies investigating changes in GAD67 protein in rats reported no differences (Basta-Kaim et al., 2015; Dick-erson et al., 2014; Osborne et al., 2019a, 2019b), which may be indicative of additional compensatory mechanisms in rats compared to mice. Only one study investigated changes in the number of GAD67 + interneurons, and reported a female specific reduction in the mPFC (Basta-Kaim et al., 2015). With respect to changes in the expression of GAD65, studies generally reported reductions in the PFC of mice (Nakamura et al., 2019; Richetto et al., 2014, 2013; Labouesse et al., 2015), which may occur due to altered maternal caregiving behaviour (Richetto et al., 2013). In contrast no differences were observed in rats (Potter et al., 2022; Duchatel et al., 2019), which may also be indicative of additional compensatory mechanisms. Changes in GAD65 protein have received limited attention, with one study reporting reduced expression in MIA exposed mice (Richetto et al., 2014). Finally, MIA was not generally associated with changes in the total amount of GABA (Winter et al., 2009; Batinić et al., 2017; Jing et al., 2013), which is reflective of inconsistent clinical findings in adult patients with ASD (Ford and Crewther, 2016; Horder et al., 2018a; Fung et al., 2021) and schizophrenia (Kumar et al., 2021; Egerton et al., 2017). Overall it can be seen in Table 1 that most studies reporting on GAD67 and GAD65 in the PFC show no changes in the juvenile and adolescent stages, but significant reductions in mRNA and protein levels in adulthood, particularly in mice and the prelimbic cortex, and to some extent the infralimbic cortex. 
3.1.7. MIA induces changes in GAD65 and GAD67 within the juvenile and adolescent hippocampus 
Only one study investigated changes in GAD67 expression in the hippocampus of adolescent mice, and reported increased GAD67 expression in MIA exposed offspring, and increased GAD65 expression in offspring raised by an MIA exposed dams (Richetto et al., 2013) (Table 2). This contrasts with studies investigating rats, which reported no differences in GAD65 or GAD67 expression or protein (Cassella et al., 2016; Rideau Batista Novais et al., 2014; Nouel et al., 2012). Similarly, the only study investigating the number of GAD67 + cells in mice reported increases within the ventral stratum oriens of 4-week-old offspring exposed to LPS, and a female specific increase in offspring exposed to Poly-I:C, with no differences observed in 2-week-old offspring or in the dCA1 (Harvey and Boksa, 2012). 3.1.8. MIA is associated with a male specific decrease in GAD67 protein within the adult hippocampus 
One study reported reduced GAD65 and GAD67 mRNA expression in the dorsal hippocampus of mice exposed to MIA (Richetto et al., 2013), however this was not replicated in two studies performed by our group (Nakamura et al., 2019, 2022) (Table 2). One rat study reported increased GAD67 mRNA expression in the granule cell layer of the DG, CA1 & CA3 (Cassella et al., 2016), while two other studies reported no differences (Potter et al., 2022; Rahman et al., 2020). Of the 5 studies investigating changes in GAD67 protein in rats, three reported reductions in male but not female offspring (Basta-Kaim et al., 2015; Osborne et al., 2019a, 2019b), which coincided with a male specific reduction in the number of GAD67 + neurons in one study (Basta-Kaim et al., 2015) (Table 2). The remaining two studies only investigated male offspring, with one reporting a reduction in GAD67 + neurons in the DG (Okano et al., 2022), and the second reporting reductions in the left dorsal hippocampus, and increases in the left ventral hippocampus of MIA exposed offspring (Dickerson et al., 2014). Of the two studies investigating GAD67 + protein in mice, one reported significant reductions in the dorsal but not ventral hippocampus of male offspring (Luoni et al., 2017), and the second reported no differences in either sex (Nakamura et al., 2019) (Table 2). Collectively, these findings suggest that MIA is likely associated with a male specific reduction in GAD67 protein in the hippocampus, which is consistent with the strong male bias in autism (Werling and Geschwind, 2013) and schizophrenia (McGrath et al., 2008), and with reports indicating greater reductions in hippocampal volume, in male schizophrenia patients (Exner et al., 2008). While we could not identify any studies comparing hippocampal GAD67 protein in male and female post-mortem tissue from people with 
ASD or schizophrenia it would be valuable to confirm if this sex specific reduction occurs clinically. 
3.1.9. MIA is associated with regionally specific changes in SST 
SST+ neurons play a key role in coordinating the activity of pyra-
midal cells at beta and theta frequencies, and synchronising theta rhythms between brain regions (Chen et al., 2017) and are substantially reduced in schizophrenia (Hashimoto et al., 2008a; Morris et al., 2008). The synchronisation of such rhythms has been proposed as a mechanism allowing for information to integrate between brain regions (Varela et al., 2001), and disruptions to beta and theta wave generation and synchrony between brain regions have been widely reported in people with schizophrenia (Uhlhaas and Singer, 2010). This has led to the suggestion that disruptions to these oscillations may underpin a wide variety of symptoms, such as hallucinations, thought disorder, and cognitive deficits (Uhlhaas and Singer, 2010), and disruptions to theta oscillations (in combination with gamma oscillations) within the PFC and hippocampus have been particularly linked to schizophrenia associated deficits in working memory (Godsil et al., 2013; Forbes et al., 2009; Lynn and Sponheim, 2016). As such, replicating the disturbances in SST+ interneurons may be of considerable importance with respect to the translational relevance of the MIA model. 
With respect to juvenile and adolescent rodents exposed to MIA, studies collectively reported no changes in SST expression or SST+ cell density in the mPFC or hippocampus (Vasistha et al., 2020; Potter et al., 2022; Okano et al., 2022; Ducharme et al., 2012; Boksa et al., 2016) (Table 3). The only study to report differences assessed changes in each cortical layer and identified a selective reduction in layer 1 of the anterior cingulate and prelimbic cortex (Lacaille et al., 2019). As the other studies did not assess layer specific differences, it is possible that layer 1 specific deficits are present following MIA which is lost when cortical layers are combined.

3.2.1. PV protein levels but not cell numbers are altered in the PFC of juvenile rodent MIA models 
The development of PV interneurons is an extended process characterised by minimal PV protein in neonatal life, which then increases steadily until adulthood (Erickson and Lewis, 2002; Paylor et al., 2016). Fourteen studies investigated the effect of MIA on PV mRNA and protein expression in the PFC of juvenile and adolescent rodents, before the conclusion of this maturational process (Table 4). One study reported a substantial but non-significant reduction in PV expression in the PFC of rats (Potter et al., 2022), while two studies reported decreases in PV staining the PrL but not IL cortex of mice (Ma et al., 2019; Matsuura et al., 2018). These findings contrast with reports investigating changes in PV+ cell density, with mouse studies generally reporting no changes in the combined PrL, cingulate and IL cortex (Lacaille et al., 2019; Vasistha et al., 2020; Paylor et al., 2016; Vojtechova et al., 2021). Studies investigating changes in the number of PV+ cells in the PFC of rats were inconsistent, with one study reporting increases in the number of PV+ cells in 2, but not 4 week old rats (Boksa et al., 2016), and a second reporting reductions (Vojtechova et al., 2021) These findings generally suggest that MIA is associated with reduced PV expression and protein in the absence of PV cell loss within the PrL cortex, reminiscent of post-mortem tissue studies from people with schizophrenia (Enwright et al., 2016). 
3.2.2. MIA is associated with disrupted PV in the adult mouse, but not rat PFC 
One study reported no differences in PV expression in the PFC of adult mice (Nakamura et al., 2019), while a second reported reduced expression (Raymann et al., 2022) (Table 4). Interestingly, the study reporting a reduction also bred MIA exposed male and female mice to study transgenerational effects, and reported no differences in PV expression in the next two generations (Raymann et al., 2022). PV protein was measured by changes in staining intensity using IHC in five studies, with four studies reporting significantly reduced staining in the PrL but not IL cortex (Ma et al., 2019; Matsuura et al., 2018; Han et al., 2016; Fujita et al., 2020; Tan et al., 2022) (Table 4). Two studies investigated changes in PV protein in the PFC using western blotting, with one study reporting reductions (Nakamura et al., 2019), and a second reporting no changes (Xia et al., 2020)(Table 4). Reductions in PV protein coincided with reductions in the number of PV+ cells, with two studies reporting a reduction in the number of PV+ cells within the combined IL and PrL cortex (Meyer et al., 2008; Wang et al., 2019), and one study reporting no changes in the cingulate cortex (Chang et al., 2020) (Table 4). While the only study using stereological techniques reported no differences in the number of PV+ cells in the PrL cortex, it is worth noting that this study used a substantially lower dose of Poly-I:C and exposed offspring to MIA in early gestation (Canetta et al., 2016), while other studies predominately investigated MIA exposure in late gestation. Furthermore, this study reported reduced GAD67 staining within PV interneurons, which is consonant with findings in post-mortem studies investigating schizophrenia (Hashimoto et al., 2003b; Curley et al., 2011). It is also notable that reductions in PV appear to follow a regionally specific pattern highly reminiscent of GAD67, with particularly evident alterations in the PrL cortex, some evidence of disruptions in the IL cortex, and no changes in the cingulate cortex. However, it is also important to note that the majority of studies assessing changes in PV protein originated from the same laboratory, 
and were the only studies to investigate mice of the DDY strain. The replication of these findings by other groups using more common strains such as the C57BL/6 is thus necessary. 
Similar to studies investigating mice, three studies investigating the rat PFC reported no differences in PV expression (Potter et al., 2022; Rahman et al., 2020; Duchatel et al., 2019) (Table 4). However, one study reported a substantial but non-significant decrease in the cingulate cortex of rats exposed at GD19 but not GD10133, while the second study reported a trend towards reduced expression (p = 0.11) at both timepoints of exposure, with notable reductions in the cingulate cortex of offspring exposed at GD19, but not GD10132. As it remains unclear if these trends represent reduced PV expression following MIA specifically in late gestation, particularly in the cingulate cortex, additional research with greater statistical power will be necessary to address this. This is especially relevant given that the only mouse study reporting no change in PV+ cell numbers assessed MIA in early gestation. 
However in stark contrast to findings in mice, MIA was not generally associated with changes in PV protein (Osborne et al., 2019a, 2019b; Gzieło et al., 2023) or PV+ cell numbers in rats (Basta-Kaim et al., 2015; Paylor et al., 2016; Baharnoori et al., 2013; Wischhof et al., 2015; Casquero-Veiga et al., 2022)(Table 4). Thus, while MIA in late gestation may reduce PV expression, rats appear less vulnerable to MIA induced alterations in PV+ cells and PV+ protein compared to mice. 
3.2.3. MIA induced alterations in PV within the hippocampus appears dependent on a complex interaction between subregion, species, sex, and gestational timepoint of exposure 
Regarding changes in PV expression in the juvenile hippocampus, one study reported no changes in the DG (Okano et al., 2022), and a second reported a female specific increase in the ventral but not dorsal region (Potter et al., 2022) (Table 5). In terms of the number or density of PV+ cells, the majority of studies reported no differences across a diverse range of regions (Okano et al., 2022; Boksa et al., 2016; Vojte-chova et al., 2021; Mao et al., 2022a) (Table 5). As such, the juvenile hippocampus may be more resilient to MIA induced dysfunctions in PV+ interneurons compared to the mPFC. 
Seven studies in adults reported no differences in PV expression in the hippocampus of mice and rats regardless of subregion (Nakamura et al., 2019, 2022; Potter et al., 2022; Rahman et al., 2020; Okano et al., 2022; Luoni et al., 2017; Raymann et al., 2022) (Table 5). However, significant reductions in PV protein were reported in the dorsal (Naka-mura et al., 2019; Luoni et al., 2017; Han et al., 2016; Nakamura et al., 2021b) but not ventral hippocampus (Nakamura et al., 2019; Luoni et al., 2017) of mice, as measured by western blotting or the staining intensity of PV+ stained IHC sections (Table 5). Only 3 studies investigated changes in PV protein in rats, which collectively reported no differences in the dorsal hippocampus (Gzieło et al., 2023) or whole hippocampal homogenate (Osborne et al., 2019a, 2019b) (Table 5). Mouse studies indicated no changes in the number of PV+ cells in the dCA1-CA3 (Meyer et al., 2008; Nakamura et al., 2021b; Giovanoli et al., 2014) regardless of gestational timepoint, while reductions in the vCA1-CA3 were reported in mice exposed to MIA in late (Meyer et al., 2008), but not early gestation (Meyer et al., 2008; Giovanoli et al., 2014) (Table 5). Reports investigating changes in PV+ cells within the rat hippocampus were more variable in their findings with respect to changes in the CA1-CA3 (Basta-Kaim et al., 2015; Dickerson et al., 2014; Wischhof et al., 2015), and future studies will be required to confirm if changes in these regions occur (Table 5). 3.2.4. MIA is associated with disruptions in GABAergic connectivity in the cortex 
In addition to changes in the total number of GABAergic interneurons, MIA was also associated with alterations in GABAergic synapses (Table 6). While Cannetta et al. reported no changes in the number of PV+ neurons, they did report reductions in efferent synaptic transmission (Canetta et al., 2016). Similarly, Okamoto et al. reported reduced synaptic transmission in the cingulate cortex which coincided with reductions in gephyrin (Okamoto et al., 2018), which plays an essential role in stabilising GABAergic synapses, is relevant to inhibitory synaptic plasticity (Choii and Ko, 2015), and has been implicated in ASD and schizophrenia (Lencz et al., 2007; Lionel et al., 2013). These functions are supported by transsynaptic interactions with neuroligin-2 and are mediated by neurexins (Poulopoulos et al., 2009; Zhang et al., 2010), which are also implicated in ASD and schizophrenia (Sun et al., 2011; Trobiani et al., 2020; Reichelt et al., 2012). Despite their potential relevance, studies investigating changes in neurexins and neuroligins are few, and have collectively reported increased expression of neurolingin-2 (Hsueh et al., 2018), and no differences in neurexin-1 (Nakamura et al., 2019, 2022) in the cortex of adult offspring exposed to MIA. Further studies investigating potential changes in gephyrin, neurexins, and neuroligins in MIA exposed offspring would be beneficial, particularly given their associations with ASD and schizophrenia. 
While it is not possible to directly measure synaptic transmission in clinical populations, post-mortem studies have indicated alterations in a range of synaptic proteins relevant to GABAergic transmission, which were often reflected in the MIA model. For instance, recent studies have reported an association between schizophrenia and reductions in vGAT (Purves-Tyson et al., 2021; Hoftman et al., 2015), particularly in PV+ interneurons (Enwright Iii et al., 2018), which were also reduced in 
mice exposed to MIA (Richetto et al., 2014). vGAT transports cytosolic GABA into presynaptic vesicles, and reductions in vGAT are associated with impairments in GABA release and synaptic transmission (Nakatsu et al., 2004; Lin et al., 2018). With respect to changes in postsynaptic signalling, no differences in the total number of GABAA receptors were reported in the MIA model (Osborne et al., 2019a, 2019b), contrasting with increases reported in schizophrenia (Verdurand et al., 2013), and decreases that arguably occur in ASD (Oblak et al., 2011, 2009; Horder et al., 2018b). While all studies investigating changes in the expression of α subunits of the GABAA receptor reported differences in adult offspring exposed to MIA, the direction of these changes is mixed, with two studies reporting reduced expression of the α2 subunit (Richetto et al., 2014, 2015), and one reporting increased expression (Rahman et al., 2020). Further research confirming decreased expression of the α2 subunit is warranted given that reduced expression of the α2 subunit in adulthood corresponded with post-mortem findings in ASD (Fatemi et al., 2009; Hong et al., 2020), and ASD associated SNP’s in the α2 subunit gene (Ma et al., 2005; Zurek et al., 2016). These changes may be driven in part by increases in MHCI molecules in early life, which was reported in the somatosensory cortex in one study identified in our search (Coiro et al., 2015), and in others that did not assess changes in GABAergic interneurons (Elmer et al., 2013; Estes et al., 2020). In-creases in MHCI has been associated with substantially reduced numbers of GABAergic and glutamatergic synapses, and a shift towards inhibition in cortical circuits (Glynn et al., 2011). Genetic polymorphisms in the MHC locus have also been associated with schizophrenia (Corvin and Morris, 2014), and altered MHCI signalling have been theorised to contribute to atypical synaptic pruning, which is in turn hypothesised to underpin multiple symptom domains (McAllister, 2014; Glausier and Lewis, 2013). As such, additional investigations focused on the potential role of MHCI may be of benefit in understanding differences in functional connectivity reported MIA exposed offspring. In summary, MIA is associated with reductions in Gephyrin, vGAT and neuroligin 2, disruptions in the expression of the α2 GABAA receptor subunit, and increases in MHCI within the cortex, however many of these differences should be confirmed in future studies.

Disruptions to inhibitory synapses at pyramidal neurons in the ventral hippocampus, and the number of inhibitory interneurons in the dorsal hippocampus are likely to disrupt the hippocampal excita-tory/inhibitory balance and promote hyperactivity. Hippocampal hyperactivity is a well replicated finding in schizophrenia (Talati et al., 2015), in those at risk of developing psychosis (Allen et al., 2018), and appears predictive of the transition to psychosis in high risk groups (Schobel et al., 2009). In brief, hyperactivity is thought to begin in the CA1 before spreading to other regions in the anterior hippocampus (Ho et al., 2017) (the ventral CA1 & subiculum in rodents), leading to the disinhibition of mesolimbic dopaminergic neurons and the emergence of psychotic symptoms (Grace, 2012; Kätzel et al., 2020). This theory has received considerable support from rodent studies, which have shown that stimulation of the vHP leads to mesolimbic hyperdopaminergia (Floresco et al., 2001; Legault et al., 2000) and that attenuation of vHP hyperactivity normalises hyperdopaminergia in the MAM model of schizophrenia (Perez et al., 2013; Gill et al., 2011). As MIA exposed mice 
also exhibit hyperdopaminergia (Luchicchi et al., 2016; Ozawa et al., 2006; Zuckerman et al., 2003), a better understanding of hippocampal GABAergic deficits in the MIA model are of particular relevance for novel therapies targeting psychotic symptoms, or the transition to psychosis. These findings collectively indicate that MIA may disrupt inhibitory input within the ventral hippocampus, and subsequently contribute to hippocampal hyperactivity and mesolimbic hyperdopaminergia. 
3.2.6. Changes in Gamma and theta oscillations in the PFC following MIA 
The PFC is thought be critical in learning and executive functioning, 
owing to its role in receiving and integrating highly processed, multimodal information from a diverse range of brain regions (Miller and Cohen, 2001). In combination with the hippocampus, the prefrontal cortex has also been functionally implicated in mediating the formation and retrieval of memories (Miller and Cohen, 2001; Jin and Maren, 2015). These functions are mediated by theta and gamma oscillations, which are thought to provide a neural code for the effective transfer of information between brain regions (Lisman and Jensen, 2013). Cogni-tive deficits are considered a core feature of schizophrenia, are lifelong, and typically emerge before the first episode of psychosis (Agnew-Blais et al., 2015). These deficits encompass a wide range of cognitive domains, and often coincide or correlate with disruptions to theta and gamma oscillations (Uhlhaas and Singer, 2010; Sohal, 2022). Given the relevance of gamma and theta oscillations in the PFC and hippocampus for schizophrenia, it is unsurprising most studies investigating gamma and theta oscillations in the MIA model focused on these regions. The lack of differences in theta power and gamma power in low stimulus contexts contrasts findings of increased baseline theta and gamma power in schizophrenia (White and Siegel, 2016; Newson and Thiagarajan, 2019), which may reflect differences between preclinical and clinical testing protocols, for example, it may be difficult to achieve a baseline reading in a mouse. In addition to task-induced and auditory-evoked disruptions in gamma and theta power, gamma and theta coherence has been reported to be reduced between the PFC and dorsal (Dickerson et al., 2014, 2010), but not ventral (Dickerson et al., 2014; Lippmann et al., 2021a) hippocampus. Gamma and theta coherence is thought to play a critical role in regulating information flow between brain regions (Benchenane et al., 2011), and coherence between the PFC and dorsal hippocampus is thought to underpin a verity of cognitive functions, including spatial navigation, memory formation, and memory consolidation (Benchenane et al., 2011). Disruptions to gamma coherence has been reported in schizophrenia, and has been suggested to functionally contribute to the formation of cognitive deficits (Wang, 2010). As such, the MIA model appears to replicate this key feature of schizophrenia. The firing of individual neurons relative to the gamma and theta cycle is also thought to play an important role in integrating information and encoding temporal information (de Almeida et al., 2009; Lisman and Buzsáki, 2008), which has been extensively studied through investigations of phase precession in hippocampal place cells. In brief, place cells fire closer to the trough of the theta cycle as an animal moves towards the cells respective place field, leading to a sequential firing of place cells which is thought to encode the immediately previous, present, and predicted future location of the animal (O’Keefe and Recce, 1993). In two studies, place cells in MIA exposed offspring have been reported to enter this procession sequence at more variable stages of the theta cycle compared to control animals, which may lead to the disorganised representations of chronological information (Speers et al., 2021a; Munn et al., 2023). Phase procession in the hippocampus, and potentially similar processes outside of the hippocampus, may contribute to sequential coding in other brain regions, including the mPFC (Jones and Wilson, 2005; Malhotra et al., 2012). Indeed, one study has also reported disrupted gamma and theta modulated activity in the mPFC of MIA exposed offspring, at the level of individual neurons (Dickerson et al., 2010). This is of particular relevance to schizophrenia, as less precise temporal processing has been reported across multiple tasks (Thoenes and Ober-feld, 2017) and has been suggested to contribute to the emergence of delusions and cognitive deficits (Andreasen et al., 1999). Overall, studies to date indicate that MIA was not generally associated with changes in baseline or low stimulus gamma or theta power, but task-induced gamma and theta power are significantly altered, particularly within the hippocampus. Furthermore, MIA was associated with reduced gamma and theta coherence between the PFC and dorsal hippocampus, and disruptions to hippocampal phase procession. It should also be noted some findings have not been presented for brevity, however we encourage the reader to consider these findings when planning future experiments (Lippmann et al., 2021a; Dickerson et al., 2010; Munn et al., 2023; Hartung et al., 2016). 
3.2.7. Behavioural phenotypes of the maternal immune activation model associate with GABAergic alterations 
In addition to recapitulating GABAergic disruptions present in schizophrenia, rodents exposed to MIA exhibited a range of memory and learning deficits, which often coincided with GABAergic abnormalities within the PFC (Lacaille et al., 2019; Richetto et al., 2014, 2013; Potter et al., 2022; Ma et al., 2019; Matsuura et al., 2018; Han et al., 2016; Fujita et al., 2020; Tan et al., 2022; Meyer et al., 2008; Chang et al., 2020; Wischhof et al., 2015; Richetto et al., 2015) and/or hippocampus (Richetto et al., 2013; Potter et al., 2022; Osborne et al., 2019a; Okano et al., 2022; Han et al., 2016; Xia et al., 2020; Meyer et al., 2008; 
Wischhof et al., 2015; Richetto et al., 2015) (Table 9). A study of particular interest was Labouesse et al., who reported that MIA-induced hypermethylation of GAD1 was negatively correlated (r = 0.51–0.57, p 
< 
0.05) with Y-maze task performance (Labouesse et al., 2015). Similarly, interventions that successfully recovered MIA induced memory deficits were also associated with the recovery of various GABAergic markers (Ma et al., 2019; Osborne et al., 2019a, 2019b; Okano et al., 2022; Han et al., 2016; Fujita et al., 2020; Tan et al., 2022; Xia et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019; Durankuş et al., 2022; Solmaz et al., 2020), with a recent study published by our laboratory also indicating that MIA associated disruptions to gamma oscillations in the CA1 of the hippocampus during two spatial memory tasks was recovered by the selective estrogen receptor modulator, raloxifene (Schroeder et al., 2019). In contrast, Richetto et al., was unable to recover working memory deficits in the MIA model following acute administration of SH-053–2’F-S-CH3. (a benzodiazepine with partial selectivity for α2, α3, and α5 containing GABAA receptors (Richetto et al., 2015)). While the authors acknowledged that this could implicate that intervention via non GABAergic mechanisms are required for the recovery of MIA induced cognitive deficits, they also noted that this negative finding may be due to SH-053–2’F-S-CH3’s high selectivity for the α5 subunit, which has been associated with cognitive impairments upon activation (Richetto et al., 2015; Gill and Grace, 2014). Overall, cognitive deficits appeared most pronounced in rodents exposed to MIA in late gestation, particularly for tests investigating changes in working memory and short-term spatial memory. This is consistent with previous reviews that did not restrict their analysis to papers assessing GABAergic deficits (Haddad et al., 2020). As MIA in mid-late gestation is associated with both disruptions to PV and SST+ interneurons and normative gamma and theta activity within the PFC and hippocampus (Nakamura et al., 2019; Dickerson et al., 2014; Schroeder et al., 2019; Dickerson et al., 2010, 2012b; Wolff and Bilkey, 2015; Lippmann et al., 2021b; Speers et al., 2021b), it is highly likely that MIA induced GABAergic deficits contributed to these late gestation specific phenotypes. 
3.2.8. The Association between MIA, Anxiety and GABAergic interneurons 
As patients with ASD and schizophrenia exhibit increased anxiety 
relative to the general population (Howells et al., 2017; McVey, 2019), we also assessed the association between MIA, GABAergic disruptions and anxiety-like behaviour. Adolescent and adult rodents exposed to MIA generally exhibited significantly increased anxiety-like behaviour (Yu et al., 2022; Cassella et al., 2016; Li et al., 2015; Vojtechova et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2019; Wischhof et al., 2015; Hsueh et al., 2018; Nyffeler et al., 2006; Mao et al., 2022b; Yang et al., 2021; Su et al., 2022; Shin Yim et al., 2017), or substantial increases that did not reach statistical significance (Lacaille et al., 2019; Potter et al., 2022; Li et al., 2018) (Table 10). Studies reporting increased anxiety-like behaviour in MIA exposed offspring also reported reduced numbers of PV+ neurons (Vojtechova et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2019; Wischhof et al., 2015), reductions in the functional transmission of inhibitory currents to their postsynaptic targets (Canetta et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2021) and reductions in total GABA (Wang et al., 2019) within the PFC, with some exceptions (Potter et al., 2022; Okano et al., 2022; Raymann et al., 2022; Wischhof et al., 2015) (Table 10). This may lead to the overvaluation of threat (Mack et al., 2022) and result in anxiety associated behaviours (Ferguson and Gao, 2018b). A functional link between anxiety-like behaviours and disrupted GABAergic signalling was provided by Yang et al., who reported that increased anxiety like behaviours coincided with reduced expression of Nav1.1 (the primary Na+ channel for GABAergic interneurons) and excessive activity in the mPFC. They then showed that excessive activity could be recovered by administering the benzodiazepine clonazepam (Yang et al., 2021), which also normalised anxiety-like behaviour (Yang et al., 2021). Another region associated with anxiety-like behaviour is the ventral hippocampus, which contains anxiety sensitive neurons that encode the presence of inherently anxiogenic contexts (Jimenez et al., 2018). While variable, studies reporting sociability deficits in MIA exposed offspring also reported reductions in GAD67 (Labouesse et al., 2015; Solmaz et al., 2020), PV protein (Fujita et al., 2020) and the number of PV+ interneurons (Braun et al., 2019; Vasistha et al., 2020; Ma et al., 2019; Vojtechova et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2019) within the PFC (Table 11). PV interneurons in the PFC play a critical role in establishing typical social behaviour (Bicks et al., 2020), and disruptions to PV interneurons may disinhibit the PFC and promote social dysfunction (Yizhar et al., 2011b). Similar to anxiety-like behaviour, Yang et al., also reported that sociability deficits coincided with excessive activity in the mPFC, which were both normalised by clonazepam (Yang et al., 2021). Similarly, Okamoto et al. reported that sociability deficits were accompanied by reduced gephyrin and disrupted inhibitory signalling within the cingulate cortex (Okamoto et al., 2018), a region that appears to be particularly relevant to social motivation and cognition (Varghese et al., 2017). Bilateral administration of clonazepam into the anterior cingulate cortex also normalised these sociability deficits (Okamoto et al., 2018). 
Further evidence linking GABAergic signalling in the mPFC with sociability deficits was provided by Labouesse et al., who reported a hypermethylation of GAD1 in the mPFC of MIA exposed mice, and a positive association between GAD1 methylation and sociability deficits (Labouesse et al., 2015). While potentiating GABAergic signalling is a promising strategy to recover social deficits in ASD and schizophrenia, pharmacological interventions will need to be highly targeted, given the diverse and ubiquitous role of GABA within the CNS. For example, while Okamoto et al. reported promising findings administering clonazepam 
into the cingulate cortex, Chabi et al. reported that muscimol (a GABAA receptor agonist) and bicuculline (a GABAA receptor antagonist) resulted in respective increases and decreases in antisocial behaviour (Chaibi et al., 2021). Similarly, Richetto et al. reported that the systemic administration SH-053–2’F-S-CH3 did not recover sociability deficits in MIA exposed mice (Richetto et al., 2015). Additionally, it is important to note that while this discussion has focused on the mPFC, the regulation of social behaviour is also associated with a range of brain regions outside of the scope of this review, such as the amygdala, nucleus accumbens, and hypothalamus (Ferrara et al., 2021). 
Another symptom of ASD and schizophrenia is disruptions to social recognition memory (Harvey and Lepage, 2014; Williams et al., 2005), that is, the capacity of an individual to remember others of their species. Social memory is an essential prerequisite for a broad range of social cognitive processes (Spreng, 2013), and is critical in maintaining the stability of social groups. While our search identified two studies reporting social memory deficits (Ma et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2019) and three studies reporting no differences (Braun et al., 2019; Lacaille et al., 2019; Richetto et al., 2015) (Table 11), reviews that did not restrict their analysis to studies investigating GABAergic markers reported more robust deficits in social memory following MIA (Bergdolt and Dunaev-sky, 2019). Unfortunately, only one study investigating MIA associated deficits in social recognition memory assessed GABAergic changes within the hippocampus and reported increased expression of the α1 and α2 subunit of the GABAA receptor in the ventral, but not dorsal hippocampus (Richetto et al., 2015) (Table 11). In summary, MIA is associated with sociability deficits that may be driven in part by disinhibition of the mPFC, while differences in social memory following MIA appears inconsistent. 3.3. The association between pre-pulse inhibition (PPI) and MIA and GABAergic interneurons 
MIA induced PPI deficits in adulthood (Han et al., 2016; Wischhof et al., 2015) but not adolescence (Deslauriers et al., 2013; Han et al., 2016; Wischhof et al., 2015), which appeared to occur in offspring exposed to MIA in early and mid (Nakamura et al., 2019; Richetto et al., 2017; Li et al., 2015; Vojtechova et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2020; Meyer et al., 2008; Chang et al., 2020; Zhang and van Praag, 2015; Vigli et al., 2020), but not late gestation (Nakamura et al., 2022; Richetto et al., 2017; Meyer et al., 2008; Nyffeler et al., 2006). The dependence of PPI deficits on the age of the offspring and the gestational timepoint of exposure is consistent with the findings of other well-known reviews that were not restricted to papers assessing GABAergic deficits (Meyer et al., 2009; Haddad et al., 2020). Disruptions to GABAergic signalling within the hippocampus may increase mesolimbic dopamine signalling via the ventral subiculum (Grace, 2010), and subsequently contribute to the development of PPI deficits (Mansbach et al., 1988) and psychotic symptoms in humans (Howes and Kapur, 2009; Kumari et al., 2008). Similarly, the subiculum lies downstream of other areas of the hippocampus proper via the CA1 and entorhinal cortex, and may become dysregulated following disruptions to other hippocampal areas. Indeed, a large number of studies reporting PPI deficits also reported deficits in either GABAergic interneurons (Nakamura et al., 2019; Potter et al., 2022; Basta-Kaim et al., 2015; Han et al., 2016; Zhang and van Praag, 2015) or GABAergic receptors (Meyer et al., 2008) within the hippocampus, and particularly implicated disruptions to PV interneurons (Nakamura et al., 2019; Potter et al., 2022; Han et al., 2016; Meyer et al., 2008; Zhang and van Praag, 2015). This aligns with reports indicating hippocampal hyperactivity in both schizophrenia (Bast and Feldon, 2003) and those at ultra-high risk of psychosis (Allen et al., 2018). Ev-idence of a functional role of hippocampal PV neurons in PPI was provided by Nyffler et al., who reported a significant negative correlation between PPI and GABAAα2 immunoreactivity within the vDG of control offspring. Interestingly, no correlation between PPI and GABAAα2 immunoreactivity was found in offspring exposed to MIA, despite MIA leading to increased GABAAα2 immunoreactivity and PPI deficits. This may indicate that residual PPI in MIA exposed offspring occurs via non-hippocampal circuits or compensatory mechanisms (Nyffeler et al., 
2006). MIA exposed offspring with disrupted PPI also frequently exhibited disruptions to SST+ and PV+ interneurons in the mPFC (Vasistha et al., 2020; Basta-Kaim et al., 2015; Vojtechova et al., 2021; Han et al., 2016; Meyer et al., 2008; Wischhof et al., 2015), which may contribute to PPI deficits by increasing dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens (Japha and Koch, 1999). Another mechanism that may disrupt PPI is disturbances in oscillatory coherence, with MIA exposed offspring exhibited disrupted low gamma coherence between the dHP and mPFC, which correlated with PPI deficits (Dickerson et al., 2010), and reduced synchronised alpha, beta, theta and gamma activity between the mPFC and vHP (Lippmann et al., 2021a) (Table 12). Similarly, we recently published that MIA-induced PPI deficits coincided with significantly reduced auditory-evoked gamma and theta oscillation power within the dorsal CA1, which coincided with significant reductions in PV and SST protein (Nakamura et al., 2019). However, while disruptions to GABAergic signalling within the ventral hippocampus and mPFC may contribute to disrupted PPI, it should be noted that additional neurotransmitters and brain regions have also been implicated in mediating PPI, which are beyond the scope of this review (Gómez-Nieto et al., 2020; Li et al., 2009). We encourage the consideration of these other regions in future studies. 
4. Conclusions and limitations 
Adult offspring exposed to MIA exhibit some comparable changes to findings observed in post-mortem tissue taken from people with schizophrenia. However, these differences appear to be highly dependent on the gestational timepoint of exposure, species used, and subregion assessed. For instance, reduced GAD67 and PV protein were reliably reported in the PFC of mice (Nakamura et al., 2019; Richetto et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2019; Luoni et al., 2017; Matsuura et al., 2018; Han et al., 2016) but not rats (Basta-Kaim et al., 2015; Dickerson et al., 2014; Osborne et al., 2019a, 2019b), and reductions in PV were particularly apparent in late gestation. While initial reports indicate that disruptions to SST+ interneurons occur in a manner similar to schizophrenia (Nakamura et al., 2019; Duchatel et al., 2016; Rahman et al., 2020), additional studies investigating changes in SST protein are needed. Changes reported in transcripts relevant to the differentiation and migration of interneurons may result in an imbalance in the number of GABAergic subtypes e.g. PV:SST disrupting the delicate balance of inhibitory input onto the axonal initial segment (in the case of PV) vs dendrites (in the case of SST). Here further studies should investigate the ratio of various interneuron subtypes in MIA models. While changes in the expression and cell density on interneuron subtypes labelled by the calcium binding protein, parvalbumin or the neuropeptide, somatostatin are consistently reported in the MIA model (see Tables 3,4 and 5), most studies showed no changes in GABA neurotransmitter (see Tables 1 and 2), suggesting MIA is more likely to impact differentiation and migration of subtypes on interneurons than overall GABA neurotransmitter levels. Finally, we also noted changes not only in cell density but mRNA expression of PV and SST, and changes in expression of GABAergic synaptic markers that could all collectively disrupt GABAergic neurotransmission and the excitatory: inhibitory balance fundamental to behaviours relevant to ASD and schizophrenia. MIA induced GABAergic deficits also frequently coincided with behavioural differences relevant to schizophrenia and ASD, and in some cases could be recovered through interventions targeting GABAergic signalling pathways. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review investigating GABAergic changes in the MIA model to date, however this review is not without limitations. For instance, as the systematic search was focused on papers investigating GABAergic disruptions in MIA exposed offspring, MIA associated behavioural deficits represent a subsection of all behavioural findings published using the MIA model. Furthermore, in order to keep the narrative focused we decided to concentrate on PV and SST GABAergic interneurons within the PFC and hippocampus, therefore the review is limited in terms of including all GABAergic subtypes across all brain regions. Nevertheless, this review provides an exhaustive summary of all GABAergic differences reported in the MIA model to date and has linked these GABAergic differences with specific behavioural domains to be used as a tool for future research. Indeed, the field has more recently moved to using the MIA model to test novel interventional treatments (Okano et al., 2022; Fujita et al., 2020; Tan et al., 2022; Xia et al., 2020; Durankuş et al., 2022; Lippmann et al., 2021a; Solmaz et al., 2020; Alizadeh et al., 2020). Furthermore, as we start unravel the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic on child neurodevelopmental outcomes (Hill et al., 2023a, 2023b) the MIA model and adapted SARS-CoV-2 MIA models will be valuable tools in investigating the validity of peripheral biomarkers of adverse neurodevelopment and testing novel interventions and therapeutics in the wake of a potential global surge in MIA-induced neurodevelopmental disorders.
